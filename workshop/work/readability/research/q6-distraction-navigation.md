# READ-1 · Distraction and navigation

## Finding

- **Graph-paper background.** No direct controlled study located tests a faint graph-paper texture behind long-form prose. Evidence from on-screen distraction studies concerns ads, notifications, or other salient stimuli, not a low-contrast static grid. Keep any grid behind text very faint or remove it from the text field as a conservative design choice; its specific effect remains unmeasured.
- **Sticky elements.** No evidence located that a sticky header or progress bar improves reading comprehension. A small Wikimedia Foundation usability study (18 readers/editors across Ghana, Indonesia, and Argentina) found positive reactions to sticky-header and table-of-contents prototypes; participants valued the contents list for understanding page structure and jumping to sections. This supports navigation utility, not comprehension gains from sticking controls to the viewport.
- **Motion near text.** Evidence is mixed for static distractions and more concerning for animation. In a 2024 eye-tracking experiment with university readers of science texts, six simple, static distractions did not significantly affect reading process, perceived load, or comprehension. The authors summarize earlier ad studies in which animated or text-adjacent ads drew attention and disrupted reading. Avoid decorative motion beside prose; do not treat the experiment as proof that every animation harms comprehension.
- **In-paragraph links.** Links introduce a choice and can draw attention. A review of 38 studies found hypertext often increases visual/decision demands and can impair performance, especially for readers with lower prior knowledge or working memory; effects depend on link structure and task ([DeStefano & LeFevre](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2005.08.012)). A web-reading experiment found linked words received different lexical processing during skim and navigable reading, and authors describe links as markers readers use to navigate ([Fitzsimmons et al.](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0239134)). Keep contextual links sparse and directly relevant; expose their destination clearly. The evidence does not justify removing useful links.
- **Bold density.** Evidence supports headings and other meaningful signals for communicating text structure, but it does not establish a maximum percentage of bold words in prose. In one controlled study, bolding negation did not improve comprehension differentially, despite readers expecting it to help ([Meyer et al.](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1004753)). This tests one targeted use, not density. Reserve bold for occasional terms whose emphasis carries meaning, rather than decorating whole clauses or paragraphs.
- **Long-read navigation.** Topic headings have controlled-study support: adult readers processed topic sentences more readily and included more topics in summaries when headings marked topic shifts ([Hyönä & Lorch](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.learninstruc.2004.01.001)); another experiment found improved memory for subtopics, not simple facts ([Lorch et al.](https://doi.org/10.1016/S1135-755X(13)70011-3)). A contents list has usability evidence for showing a page's structure and enabling direct jumps ([Wikimedia Foundation testing](https://www.mediawiki.org/wiki/Reading/Web/Desktop_Improvements/Repository/Sticky_Header_and_Table_of_Contents_User_Testing)). No direct evidence located that a reading-progress bar by itself improves comprehension. Use semantic chapter landmarks and a contents list where length warrants; progress cues are optional orientation feedback.

## Evidence and quality

- **Controlled study / eye tracking:** Ronconi et al. tested passive, simple on-screen distractions during reading and reported no significant comprehension or cognitive-load effect in their sample; the paper also reviews mixed findings on ads, with animation and proximity to text associated with more attention in cited studies. This is adjacent evidence, not a graph-paper test. [Ronconi et al., *Effects of Digital Reading With On-Screen Distractions*](https://doi.org/10.1111/jcal.13106)
- **Review of controlled hypertext studies:** DeStefano and LeFevre reviewed 38 studies and concluded that additional link decisions and visual processing can impair performance, with meaningful individual-difference and structure effects. [DeStefano & LeFevre, *Cognitive Load in Hypertext Reading: A Review*](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2005.08.012)
- **Controlled online reading experiments:** A study comparing linked and unlinked text during comprehension and skimming/navigation found that hyperlinks influenced lexical processing and were used as information markers for navigation; this moderates a blanket “links distract” rule. [Fitzsimmons et al., PLOS ONE](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0239134)
- **Controlled studies of text structure:** Lorch, Lemarié, and Chen found headings improved memory for subtopics versus equivalent preview sentences, while recall of simple facts did not differ. [*Signaling topic structure via headings or preview sentences*](https://doi.org/10.1016/S1135-755X(13)70011-3). Hyönä and Lorch found headings facilitated processing of topic sentences and increased topics included in readers’ summaries. [*Effects of topic headings on text processing*](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.learninstruc.2004.01.001)
- **Controlled bolding study:** Meyer et al. found that emphasizing negation with bold did not produce the expected comprehension advantage. This is narrow evidence and cannot establish a general optimal bold density. [ERIC record](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1004753)
- **Expert usability research, not comprehension testing:** Wikimedia Foundation prototype testing involved 18 readers/editors across three regions. Participants reported that a contents list helped them understand page structure and navigate; the study did not measure learning or comprehension. [Wikimedia Foundation, sticky header and table-of-contents user testing](https://www.mediawiki.org/wiki/Reading/Web/Desktop_Improvements/Repository/Sticky_Header_and_Table_of_Contents_User_Testing)
- **Evidence gap:** I found no direct reading-performance study of graph-paper backgrounds, persistent progress indicators, paragraph-level bold density, or sticky UI occlusion during long-form reading. Those choices below are cautious implementation judgments informed by adjacent findings and standard web usability, not proven comprehension interventions.

## Limits

The distraction experiment used two science texts and simple, static, peripheral stimuli; it does not test animated site chrome, textured backgrounds, or the reading layout. Hypertext results vary by reader, task, and link design, and much of the review predates current web patterns. Heading experiments test structured expository texts, not legal lessons specifically. The Wikimedia study is small and qualitative. No causal comprehension claim should be attached to progress bars or sticky controls from this evidence.

## CSS implication

- Keep the grid low-contrast beneath prose, or confine it to margins; treat either choice as a visual-design hypothesis for the specimen, not a research-backed comprehension rule.
- Keep navigation out of the reading line where possible. Prefer a compact, optional contents control or static contents list over a tall persistent header; if a sticky control is tried, keep it short and check that it does not cover anchored headings (`scroll-margin-block-start`).
- Avoid looping, flashing, or auto-starting motion beside prose. Keep transitions restrained and respect `prefers-reduced-motion`.
- Style inline links consistently and visibly, and keep link placement tied to the sentence's subject. Avoid turning every key term into a navigation decision.
- Use bold sparingly and semantically; use real heading levels for section structure rather than using bold paragraphs as faux headings.
- Add descriptive chapter headings and a table of contents for long lessons. A thin progress cue may be tested as orientation feedback, with no claim that it improves comprehension.
