## A1. Reader brief

- **Already knows:** the usual distinctions between diffuse and concentrated control, including the contrast between incidental review in an individual case and principal review in an abstract action (Aula 11). Can identify a constitutional issue and the court deciding it.
- **Can do after reading** (tasks a question could ask, not topics):
  1. Given a diffuse STF decision and art. 52, X, distinguish what the judgment decides for the parties from the Senate’s textual power and explain what a Senate resolution does.
  2. Assess the professor’s criticism of combining ordinary jurisdiction with constitutional review by identifying its claimed benefit and the risk of conflicting judgments.
  3. Given a claim that diffuse review has been “abstractivized,” distinguish the mutation thesis, its criticism, and the narrower evidence of expanded effects in later STF cases.
  4. Explain why general reach, binding force, and the formal status of the statute must not be collapsed into one consequence.
- **One sitting:** about 3,200 words, about 20 minutes.

**Retrieval contract:** A2 traps T1–T4 are each defused once in the named section and tested once in the closing quiz. The worked case tests T3/T4 through the real procedural history of HC 82.959 and Rcl 4.335, adding the distinction between outcome and rationale.

## A8. Cut list (and why)

- Definitions of diffuse/concentrated control, their traditional procedural pairing, and their ordinary effects: recap only as needed for the argument; Aula 11 owns the full distinction.
- Full Kelsen–Schmitt debate and the institutional theory of the constitutional court: Aula 03 owns it; this lesson considers only the professor’s specific argument about cumulation.
- General doctrine of binding precedent, repercussion general, reservation of plenary, and the procedural operation of reclamação: Aula 36 owns the detail. Here, SV 26 is only the fact needed to understand Rcl 4.335.
- Exhaustive history of art. 52, X from earlier constitutions and every Senate resolution: the lesson needs the text, the conventional function, and the concrete interpretive dispute, not a constitutional history.
- Backlash and subsequent legislative reaction: Aula 18 owns it.
- Any claim that all diffuse judgments now have automatic general and binding effects: the cited sources support specific developments and contested readings, not that unqualified statement.
- The live page’s broad foreign-model survey and repeated rhetorical question sequence: they are not needed to meet the S0 outcome; keep the material on Brazilian coexistence and its consequences.
