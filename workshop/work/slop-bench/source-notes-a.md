# SLOP-2 source notes — research A

## 1. SlopBench (Roongta, Gaddipati & Huynh, arXiv:2609.33905, 27 Sep 2026)

**Claims verified.** The benchmark evaluates 18 models on 112 human-written prompts in four English writing domains, with 19,928 outputs total. Its four measurable surface dimensions are task-relative length, repeated openers across samples of the same task, paragraph rhythm, and fixed lexical constructions against pre-ChatGPT human corpora. Its abstract reports Kimi K2.6 at 21.1 and Mistral Large at 40.6 under one weighting; across 500 random reweightings those were the lowest/highest in 58%/97% of draws. No draw preserves the full 18-model ordering, and the paper says the composite is one weighting among many. The authors also report no observed association with crowd Elo (Pearson r = −0.115, 95% CI [−0.55, +0.37], p=.65; n=18 models), with wide uncertainty.

**Recommendation: ADAPT, narrowly.** Borrow the principle of checking observable dimensions separately and calibrating length to the requested task. For this gate, that means repeated templates, needless repetition, empty paragraph rhythm, and requested-length fit can be useful signals. Do not import its English tells, reference corpora, weights, model rankings, or numeric cutoffs into Portuguese legal-teaching prose. The study itself limits “surface slop” to style and excludes truth, reasoning, coherence, and usefulness; it also reports instability under weights/domains and poor crowd confirmation. Legal accuracy and pedagogical utility need their own review criteria, not a blended slop score.

**Primary evidence:** [arXiv abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.33905); [paper HTML, methods and limitations](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.33905), especially §§1, 4, 6.5–6.7.

## 2. Shaib et al., “Measuring AI ‘Slop’ in Text” (arXiv:2509.19163v2, 24 Jan 2026)

**Claims verified.** The paper builds a taxonomy from 19 expert definitions and annotates spans in 150 news articles and 100 QA passages. The taxonomy covers information utility (density, relevance), information quality (factuality, bias), structure (repetition, templatedness), coherence, and tone-related features (fluency, verbosity, word complexity, tone). Binary document-level “slop” judgments showed low pairwise Cohen’s κ (−.15, .29, .06); prevalence-adjusted Gwet AC1 values were .12, .42, .28. The authors emphasize variation by domain: factuality and structural issues were strongest for short QA passages, while news judgments included style and utility issues. They state fully automated scalable measurement remains an open challenge; capable reasoning LLMs also failed to reliably identify slop.

**Recommendation: ADOPT the multidimensional framing; ADAPT its operationalization.** For a Portuguese legal-teaching gate, retain factuality, relevance, information density, repetition/templatedness, coherence, and task-appropriate tone as distinct questions. Do not reduce them to one binary “slop” label or claim that an automated detector can settle them. The paper’s annotators and corpora are English news/QA, not legal teaching; agreement is especially weak for the holistic binary judgment. Translate the categories into locally reviewed criteria and preserve human judgment for claims, reasoning, and instructional fit.

**Primary evidence:** [arXiv abstract/version record](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.19163); [full paper HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.19163), §§3–6 (taxonomy, sample, agreement, domain results, automation limits).

## 3. Paech et al., “Antislop: A Comprehensive Framework for Identifying and Eliminating Repetitive Patterns in Language Models” (ICLR 2026)

**Claims verified.** The ICLR proceedings abstract reports the sampler can suppress 8,000+ patterns while maintaining quality, versus token banning becoming unusable at 2,000, and reports FTPO achieving 90% slop reduction while maintaining/improving cross-domain evaluations. The paper’s own abstract gives context: patterns over 1,000× more frequent than its human baseline; its experiments use Gemma 3 12B and English creative-writing prompts, including 1,000 held-out outputs per condition. In the main results, the sampler gives 100% banlist suppression and a higher rubric score than baseline on that experiment; FTPO yields 83–92% suppression with quality within 1% of baseline. The methods use backtracking and configurable soft suppression; the appendix demonstrates a contextually required suppressed term can still be generated. The evidence is not a test of Portuguese legal writing or a general-purpose classroom rubric.

**Recommendation: ADAPT the caution, REJECT the banlist as a writing gate.** The useful lesson is that suppressing repeated surface patterns need not mean treating every occurrence as wrong: the paper explicitly supports soft bans and contextual exceptions. A legal-writing gate should flag possible habitual filler/constructions for contextual review, never ban legally meaningful words or phrases globally. Do not cite the paper’s model-training gains as evidence that our human editorial gate will improve quality by the same percentages.

**Primary evidence:** [ICLR 2026 proceedings abstract](https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2026/hash/467746c8e15fbfca34dcf23be9ef9229-Abstract-Conference.html); [paper PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.15061), abstract, §3 (English creative-writing baseline), §§4–6 (sampler and evaluations), Appendix A (soft banning).

### Companion implementations (distinct artifacts)

- **Auto-Antislop:** [official repository](https://github.com/sam-paech/auto-antislop). Its README describes a pipeline that samples a specified prompt set, identifies model-specific overrepresented words/phrases/n-grams, builds preference data, and fine-tunes using FTPO. **Recommendation: REJECT as a direct writing-gate component.** This is a model profiling/training pipeline, not an editorial rubric; its learned patterns are model- and prompt-set-dependent. Its conceptual model-specific profiling can inform later Portuguese corpus research, but would require a purpose-built corpus and evaluation.
- **Antislop Sampler:** [official repository](https://github.com/sam-paech/antislop-sampler). The repository supplies sampler code and English phrase/word/regex lists; the paper describes backtracking after a pattern match and soft as well as hard suppression. **Recommendation: REJECT the supplied lists/runtime as a direct gate; ADAPT only the idea of context-sensitive flags.** Static English lists do not establish Portuguese legal-writing problems, and even in the paper’s design a flagged term may be necessary in context. Keep any gate signal explainable and reviewable rather than suppressing tokens.
